AI Readiness Workbook

AI Readiness for Creative and Cultural Organisations

A self-serve diagnostic for executive teams, senior management and boards in creative and cultural organisations. Score your organisation, interpret the result, and agree what to do next.

01

Introduction

This workbook gives your organisation a practical framework for understanding its current exposure to AI, identifying opportunities, and deciding what controls and next steps are needed. It is designed for executive teams, senior management and boards in creative and cultural organisations.

It can be completed by one person, used in a workshop, or taken to a board meeting. The workbook is free to use, share and adapt internally. Please keep the Creative Thinking Systems attribution intact.

02

Why AI Readiness Matters Now

AI is already entering creative and cultural organisations through everyday tools, staff experimentation, suppliers, partners, audiences and creative workflows.

Advances in AI are increasing risks around cybersecurity, intellectual property, personal data and confidential information. At the same time, AI can support more efficient ways of operating, managing and communicating in the cultural sector.

Being AI ready means knowing:

  • 01Where AI is already being used across your organisation.
  • 02Which activities create data, IP, copyright, confidentiality or reputational risk.
  • 03Which workflows could benefit from safe AI support.
  • 04Which tools are approved for staff use.
  • 05What information should never be entered into public AI tools.
  • 06Who remains accountable for AI-assisted outputs.
  • 07How benefits and risks will be measured.
03

Current Exposure and Risk Areas

Your organisation is likely already exposed to AI-related risk before it has formally adopted an AI strategy or issued organisation-wide guidance. A priority is to understand where AI may already be in use, where that use creates exposure, and what controls are needed.

You should know whether staff, suppliers or partners are using AI in the following areas, because these uses may involve personal data, confidential information, copyrighted material, public-facing content or operational decisions.

The examples below do not imply confirmed non-compliance. They identify where AI use could create GDPR, IP, confidentiality, cybersecurity, fraud or reputational exposure if unmanaged.

01
Staff using public AI tools with internal material

Board papers, HR documents, contracts, financial information, strategy notes or internal reports may be copied into public AI tools for drafting, summarising or rewriting.

Main risks

Confidential information exposure; personal data processing without safeguards; loss of control over sensitive organisational material.

02
Audience, donor or CRM data being analysed with AI

Staff may use AI to analyse survey responses, audience feedback, donor notes, booking patterns or stakeholder information.

Main risks

GDPR exposure; donor and audience trust risk; inaccurate or biased analysis.

03
Creative and production materials being entered into AI tools

Scripts, recordings, exhibition assets, design references, production documentation, archive material, artists’ images or commissioned works may be used in AI tools.

Main risks

Copyright or licence breach; unclear ownership of AI-assisted outputs; lack of artist or contributor consent; attribution issues; reputational risk with artists, writers, performers and rights holders.

04
AI transcription, marketing or supplier tools operating without review

AI meeting summaries, marketing generators, translation tools, ticketing platforms, CRM systems or email tools may already be processing your data through embedded AI features.

Main risks

Unknown data processing, retention or reuse; supplier terms may not meet your requirements; confidential meetings may be recorded or summarised without proper notice.

05
AI-enabled cybersecurity and fraud exposure

AI may be used to create convincing phishing emails, fake invoices, malicious links, synthetic voice messages, impersonation of senior staff or suppliers, or attacks on email accounts, the website or key IT systems.

Main risks

Financial loss; payment fraud; system compromise; data breach; operational disruption; reputational damage; loss of trust with suppliers, donors, audiences or artists.

The Diagnostic

Use this to assess your organisation’s current AI maturity. Score each area 1 to 5.

Maturity scale

  • 1No clear position. Use is unknown or unmanaged.
  • 2Informal experimentation. Inconsistent controls.
  • 3Basic guidance exists. Useful use cases identified.
  • 4Managed adoption. Clear ownership, tools, training and controls.
  • 5Integrated practice. Benefits and risks are actively measured.

How to Interpret the Scores

Mostly 1s and 2s. Your organisation has unmanaged exposure. The priority is control:
  • Map current AI use
  • Issue interim guidance
  • Identify approved tools
  • Review data and confidentiality risks
  • Add AI to the risk register
Mostly 3s. Your organisation has started to respond. The priority is structure:
  • Map workflows
  • Train staff
  • Select pilot use cases
  • Define review and approval processes
  • Report progress to leadership and the board
Mostly 4s and 5s. Your organisation is ready to scale adoption. The priority is improvement:
  • Measure benefits
  • Refine governance
  • Expand safe use cases
  • Improve knowledge management
  • Embed AI into priority workflows

What Success Looks Like

An AI-ready organisation does not replace people with technology. It uses AI to reduce friction, improve coordination, strengthen governance and give staff more time for judgement, relationships and delivery.

Administration.

AI reduces repetitive drafting, formatting, summarising and coordination work. First drafts of reports, briefings, policies and funding materials are produced faster, drawing more effectively on existing knowledge and data. Meeting notes, actions and summaries become easier to produce and share. Teams reduce duplication across departments.

Risk and governance.

Your organisation knows where AI is being used and applies proportionate controls. AI appears on the organisational risk register. Staff understand what data must not be entered into AI tools. Approved tools and prohibited uses are clearly defined. Supplier AI features are reviewed. Public-facing AI-assisted content is checked before release.

Operations and delivery.

AI supports planning, documentation, handovers and reporting. Operational teams spend less time creating and reworking routine documentation. Risk assessments, access notes and technical checklists become more consistent. Project reports and post-event learning are easier to capture and share.

Fundraising and development.

AI supports research, drafting, stakeholder mapping and impact reporting. Donor, trust and stakeholder research becomes faster. Funding applications and impact reports move from blank page to review draft more quickly. Funder-facing claims remain accurate, evidenced and human-reviewed.

Marketing and audience development.

AI supports campaign development, audience insight, accessibility and communication. Marketing teams create more campaign variants without losing their organisation’s voice. Audience feedback and survey data are analysed more consistently. Accessibility, plain-English and multilingual materials become easier to produce.

People and creative judgement.

AI supports staff, artists and leadership without replacing judgement. Staff understand when to use AI and when not to. Final decisions remain with accountable people. Artistic, contractual, safeguarding and reputational decisions stay human-led. Artists and creative teams understand how AI may or may not be used in their work.

Knowledge and archive.

Your organisation makes better use of its existing knowledge, archive and institutional memory. Teams can draw on previous reports, templates, policies and project records more easily. New staff and partners can access clearer onboarding and background material. Past projects, audience insights and funding materials inform future work.

What to Do Next

Take a staged approach. Establish control, identify value, pilot and measure.

First 30 days : Establish control
Actions
  • Map current AI use across staff and partners.
  • Identify high-risk information, including audience, donor, HR, artist, contract, board and financial data.
  • Identify any immediate GDPR, confidentiality or cyber concerns.
  • Add AI to the organisational risk register.
  • Define approved and non-approved tools.
  • Issue interim AI usage guidance.
Output: Current exposure map and interim AI guidance.
Days 30 to 60 : Identify value
Actions
  • Map recurring workflows across operations, fundraising, marketing, programming and governance.
  • Identify three to five low-risk, high-utility pilot use cases.
  • Agree approval rules for public-facing AI-assisted material.
  • Identify training needs for staff and managers.
Output: Prioritised AI opportunity map and pilot plan.
Days 60 to 90 : Pilot and measure
Actions
  • Train pilot users and agree use-case owners.
  • Test AI in selected workflows.
  • Track time saved, quality improved and risks identified.
  • Review staff confidence and adoption.
  • Capture lessons and refine guidance.
  • Report findings to the executive team and board.
  • Decide whether to scale, pause or redesign each pilot.
Output: AI readiness update report and next-stage adoption plan.
